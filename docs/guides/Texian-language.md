# A Working Glossary of Texian Words and Terms

## Don’tch’ya

A compressed Texian construction meaning “do you not?”

It is useful when the answer is already obvious and the speaker is merely giving the listener one last opportunity to catch up.

Example:

“Don’tch’ya know that gate’s been dragging since Tuesday?”

## Durn tootin’

An emphatic declaration of agreement.

Stronger than “yes,” more trustworthy than “absolutely,” and usually delivered with enough confidence to end the discussion.

Example:

“You reckon that’ll hold?”

“Durn tootin’.”

## Yes’siree

A polished form of enthusiastic agreement.

It carries manners, confidence, and just enough ceremony to sound respectable without getting above its raising.

Example:

“You want another slice?”

“Yes’siree.”

## Y’all

The proper second-person plural pronoun English forgot to finish inventing.

It may refer to two people, twenty people, or one person accompanied by several opinions.

Example:

“Y’all come on inside.”

## All y’all

Every last person in the addressed group, including anyone pretending the statement does not apply to them.

Example:

“All y’all need to quit leaning on that fence.”

## Fixin’ to

Preparing or intending to do something.

The action may occur immediately, shortly, eventually, or after one more cup of coffee.

Example:

“I’m fixin’ to mow that back field.”

## Might could

An expression of cautious possibility.

It means something may be possible, may be advisable, and may become somebody else’s problem if handled incorrectly.

Example:

“We might could patch that until Monday.”

## Reckon

To think, suppose, estimate, conclude, or arrive at a judgment after sufficient observation.

Example:

“I reckon that storm’s headed east.”

## Ain’t

A highly adaptable grammatical tool covering several forms of negation.

Its usefulness greatly exceeds the respectability assigned to it by people who write grammar books indoors.

Example:

“That ain’t going to fit.”

## Ain’t no

An intensified negative construction.

It often appears in statements where the speaker has no intention of entertaining objections.

Example:

“There ain’t no sense in doing it twice.”

## Bless your heart

A phrase whose meaning depends entirely on tone, timing, facial expression, and the severity of the mistake under discussion.

It may express sincere sympathy.

It may also mean the speaker has identified a catastrophic shortage of judgment.

Example:

“You wired the whole shed backward? Bless your heart.”

## Over yonder

A location understood perfectly by the speaker and expected to become obvious to everyone else after one vague hand gesture.

Example:

“Set it over yonder by the trailer.”

## Cattywampus

Crooked, misaligned, uneven, poorly arranged, or generally sitting in a condition offensive to the eye.

Example:

“That whole porch is cattywampus.”

## Whopperjawed

Knocked badly out of alignment.

Often used for machinery, doors, framing, vehicles, or people who have been physically or emotionally rearranged.

Example:

“That hinge is all whopperjawed.”

## Tumped over

Fell over, tipped over, or was caused to lose its upright condition.

Example:

“The wheelbarrow tumped over in the ditch.”

## Whole ’nother

An entirely separate category, situation, problem, or level of foolishness.

It is grammatically suspicious and semantically flawless.

Example:

“That wiring issue is a whole ’nother problem.”

## Imma

A compact form of “I am going to.”

It carries intention and momentum and generally suggests the decision has already been made.

Example:

“Imma go check that pump.”

## Ought to

Should do, would be wise to do, or had better do before circumstances become less friendly.

Commonly pronounced with extra weight and occasionally written as “’aught,” though “ought” is the standard form.

Example:

“You ought to shut that valve first.”

## Skeered

Afraid.

Frequently used while accusing somebody else of fear, especially when nobody involved is actually afraid.

Example:

“What’s wrong? You skeered?”

## By gosh

A restrained expression of surprise, admiration, or realization.

Suitable for company where stronger language might upset somebody’s aunt.

Example:

“By gosh, it finally started.”

## Well, I’ll be damned

A declaration that new information has successfully breached the speaker’s defenses.

Example:

“Well, I’ll be damned. The old thing still runs.”

## How ’bout that

An expression of surprise, approval, satisfaction, or mild disbelief.

The exact meaning is carried mostly by eyebrow position.

Example:

“How ’bout that? It fit the first time.”

## Ain’t no flies on that

A statement that something is sharp, sound, efficient, current, or otherwise too lively for flies to settle on.

Example:

“That little motor pulls good. Ain’t no flies on that.”

## You couldn’t get it no plummer

You could not make it any straighter, clearer, more level, or more plainly true.

“Plummer” here refers to plumb, as in perfectly vertical or properly aligned.

Example:

“That explanation is dead on. You couldn’t get it no plummer.”

## Carry on

Continue what you are doing.

Depending on tone, it may indicate approval, resignation, dismissal, or a decision not to interfere with somebody learning a lesson the hard way.

Example:

“You already cut it twice and it’s still short? Carry on.”

## Now hold on

Stop immediately before the current idea becomes more expensive.

Example:

“Now hold on. You’re fixing to cut the load-bearing post.”

## Hush now

Stop talking.

It may be gentle, affectionate, corrective, or the final warning before patience leaves the premises.

Example:

“Hush now and listen.”

## Quit piddlin’

Stop wasting time on low-value activity.

Example:

“Quit piddlin’ with that radio and help me load this.”

## Git-r-done

Complete the work.

The phrase emphasizes completion over ceremony, though it should not be interpreted as permission to perform unsafe or foolish work.

Example:

“We know what needs doing. Let’s git-r-done.”

## That’ll work

The result is adequate, functional, approved, and not worth another meeting.

Example:

“Bolt clears the housing now.”

“That’ll work.”

## That’ll technically work

A warning disguised as approval.

It means the proposed method may function briefly while quietly arranging a future failure.

Example:

“You can hard-code the URL.”

“That’ll technically work.”

## Good enough for who it’s for

The quality level has been judged appropriate for the intended audience or purpose.

Sometimes practical.

Sometimes suspicious.

Example:

“The paint’s thin on the back side, but it’s good enough for who it’s for.”

## Good enough for the gals I go out with

A humorous declaration that additional refinement would not improve the speaker’s practical results.

Example:

“You going to polish that weld?”

“Nah. Good enough for the gals I go out with.”

## Ain’t my first rodeo

The speaker has prior experience with the situation and does not require elementary instruction.

Example:

“You know how to back that trailer?”

“Ain’t my first rodeo.”

## That dog won’t hunt

The idea, plan, excuse, or explanation is not viable.

Example:

“You want to fix the leak with paint? That dog won’t hunt.”

## Too big for your britches

Acting beyond one’s judgment, authority, experience, or proven ability.

Example:

“He’s been here two days and already wants to run the place. Too big for his britches.”

## Straighten up and fly right

Correct your behavior, attitude, or direction.

Example:

“You boys straighten up and fly right.”

## Raised right

Taught manners, responsibility, practical sense, and the proper treatment of other people.

Example:

“She brought the tools back cleaner than she found them. Raised right.”

## Much obliged

Thank you.

More durable than “thanks” and often used when somebody has genuinely helped.

Example:

“Appreciate the lift. Much obliged.”

## Yessum

A respectful form of “yes, ma’am.”

Example:

“Did you bring the groceries?”

“Yessum.”

## No’m

A respectful form of “no, ma’am.”

Example:

“Did you track mud in here?”

“No’m.”

## Come hell or high water

Regardless of obstacles, danger, delay, weather, expense, or management interference.

Example:

“We’ll finish that roof today, come hell or high water.”

## Raised Cain

Caused a disturbance, objected loudly, or created considerable commotion.

Example:

“He raised Cain when they moved his truck.”

## Full as a tick

Extremely full after eating.

Example:

“I couldn’t eat another bite. I’m full as a tick.”

## Fine as frog hair

Exceptionally fine, delicate, or excellent.

Frog hair does not need to exist for the phrase to remain useful.

Example:

“That adjustment is fine as frog hair.”

## Hotter than a two-dollar pistol

Extremely hot, lively, fast, fashionable, or agitated.

Example:

“That skillet’s hotter than a two-dollar pistol.”

## Nervous as a long-tailed cat in a room full of rocking chairs

Extremely nervous for understandable reasons.

Example:

“He’s nervous as a long-tailed cat in a room full of rocking chairs.”

## Busier than a one-legged man in a butt-kicking contest

Overwhelmed with activity under unfavorable conditions.

Example:

“I’ve been busier than a one-legged man in a butt-kicking contest.”

## Useless as tits on a boar hog

Completely without practical value.

Crude, vivid, and not intended for formal correspondence.

Example:

“That stripped screwdriver is useless as tits on a boar hog.”

## Not worth the powder to blow it to hell

So lacking in value that even destroying it would be an unjustified expense.

Example:

“That old pump ain’t worth the powder to blow it to hell.”

## More nerve than a bum tooth

Excessively bold, irritating, or shameless.

Example:

“He borrowed my truck and complained about the gas. More nerve than a bum tooth.”

## Rode hard and put up wet

Showing severe wear, exhaustion, neglect, or poor maintenance.

The phrase comes from mistreating a horse by failing to cool and care for it after hard work.

Example:

“That truck looks rode hard and put up wet.”

## Sweating like a whore in church

Extremely nervous, uncomfortable, guilty, overheated, or all four.

Crude and best reserved for company that will understand the spirit of the phrase.

Example:

“He was sweating like a whore in church when the sheriff walked in.”

## Stand flat-footed and hump a football

A forceful instruction to go attempt something absurd and leave the speaker alone.

Used when a demand, suggestion, or attitude has exhausted available patience.

Example:

“You want me to redo it for free? Go stand flat-footed and hump a football.”

## Crap flows downhill

Problems, blame, extra work, and bad decisions tend to move from people with authority toward people with less power to avoid them.

Hydraulically plausible and organizationally proven.

Example:

“Management changed the deadline again. Crap flows downhill.”

## Chicken ain’t nothing but a bird

Do not overcomplicate, overvalue, or overdramatize the matter.

The situation is simpler than somebody is making it sound.

Example:

“Quit acting like supper is a state occasion. Chicken ain’t nothing but a bird.”

## He needed killin’

Traditional hyperbole indicating that somebody was profoundly disliked or had behaved intolerably.

It is not literal advice, legal guidance, or an acceptable justification for violence.

Example:

“Why was everybody mad at him?”

“Well, folks said he needed killin’.”

## Puddle hopping

Moving around the edges of a subject instead of addressing it directly.

It may also describe jumping between small tasks without completing the main one.

Example:

“Quit puddle hopping and tell me what broke.”

## Back 40

The distant or less frequently visited part of a property.

More broadly, it can mean the far reaches of a project, topic, system, or conversation.

Example:

“We covered everything from the front gate to the back 40.”

## Ya hear?

A closing phrase used to confirm that an instruction, warning, or point has been understood.

Usually rhetorical.

Example:

“Don’t leave that gate open again, ya hear?”

## Rapido

Quickly.

Borrowed from Spanish and commonly used in informal speech when ordinary urgency does not seem urgent enough.

Example:

“Get that tarp over the hay, rapido.”

## You durn tootin’

A final, emphatic confirmation that the matter is settled and no further analysis is required.

Example:

“You reckon that’s Texian?”

“You durn tootin’.”

## Paint me red like the tick on a dog named Fred

An expression of surprise, disbelief, embarrassment, or sudden realization.

It is usually delivered after discovering something unexpected, especially when the speaker has just corrected themselves or been caught off guard.

Example:

“Paint me red like the tick on a dog named Fred—I left the keys in the truck.”
